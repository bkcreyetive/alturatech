Why expansion revenue has no owner in most B2B orgs

October 15, 2026 · Alturatech

Ask a B2B SaaS company who owns new-logo revenue and the answer comes immediately: sales. Ask who owns expansion revenue — upsell, cross-sell, seat growth inside an existing account — and the answer gets vague fast. Customer success flags the signal, sales closes the paperwork if someone remembers to loop them in, and product ships the feature that made the expansion possible without ever seeing a dollar figure attached to it. Three functions touch the motion. None is accountable for whether it happens.

Net revenue retention gets reported as a single board number, which is exactly why the gap stays invisible. A blended 110% can mask a handful of large accounts expanding nicely while the rest of the base quietly contracts — and a healthy aggregate gives leadership no reason to ask who's driving the accounts that aren't expanding.

Why the handoff breaks by design, not by accident

Each function's incentives point somewhere other than expansion. Sales is compensated on new logos because that's the number easiest to forecast and tie to quota. Customer success is measured on retention — keeping the account from churning — which is a defensive metric, not a growth one; a CSM who spends the quarter chasing an upsell instead of shoring up a renewal risk is optimizing against their own scorecard. Product ships usage data into a dashboard customer success might glance at, but that data was never built to answer "which accounts are ready to expand right now," because nobody asked product to build for that question.

The result is a motion that only happens when someone personally decides to go out of their way — a CSM who flags an account unprompted, a seller who notices a usage spike and follows up without being told to. That's not a process. It's a habit a few good people happen to have, and it evaporates the moment they leave or get buried in quota-bearing work that's actually measured.

What ownership actually requires

Fixing this isn't about adding a fourth function or a new title. It's about picking which existing function is accountable for the number and giving them the authority and the data to act on it. Three things have to exist together, and most orgs have at most one: a named owner whose comp plan includes expansion revenue, not just new logos or gross retention; account-level instrumentation that surfaces expansion-readiness signals — approaching a seat cap, a workflow used at high frequency, a feature adopted by one team but not the rest of the account — rather than aggregate product analytics nobody operationalizes; and a defined trigger-to-action path, so a signal turns into a specific person reaching out within a specific window instead of sitting in a dashboard unnoticed.

The instrumentation piece is usually the one that's missing, because it requires product and revenue teams to agree on what an expansion signal looks like before an engineer builds anything — and that conversation rarely happens until someone senior asks why NRR has been flat for two quarters despite the logo count going up.

The segmentation most teams skip

Not every account should be chased the same way, and treating them as one bucket is how the motion ends up too diffuse for anyone to own. An account near its usage ceiling with an engaged economic buyer is a different motion — faster, higher-touch — from an account with healthy usage but no champion above the practitioner level. The first is a sales-led conversation that can happen this quarter. The second needs a relationship built before there's anything to sell, on a longer clock. Collapsing both into one "expansion pipeline" number is how a fast-moving opportunity gets the same cadence as a slow relationship-building effort, and both end up moving slower than they should.

None of this requires a reorg. It requires someone to decide, explicitly, that expansion revenue is a line item with an owner rather than a byproduct of goodwill — and to build the instrumentation and the incentive structure that make the ownership real instead of nominal. Most companies find this out the same way: a board member asks why NRR has been flat for two quarters, and the honest answer turns out to be that nobody was ever actually driving it.
